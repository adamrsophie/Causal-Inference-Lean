Background and glossary

The terms used across this site, each with a definition, a small example, and a link to where the library defines or uses it. Every numerical example on this page is recomputed with exact rational arithmetic by a script that ships with the site generator.

Notation: Y(1) and Y(0) are potential outcomes, A or D a binary treatment, Z an instrument, E the mean, and P a probability. The library writes potential outcomes as functions: for example Y z d in the instrumental variable model, or the vectors y1 y0 : ι → ℝ of a finite population.

Glossary: potential outcomes · causal effects · identification, estimation, inference · identified set · ignorability and positivity · design-based inference · sharp null · instrument and compliers · parallel trends · regression discontinuity · synthetic control · interference · mediation · missing data · sensitivity analysis · causal graph · intervention and surgery · d-separation · mixed graphs and districts · latent projection · moral graph · back-door and front-door criteria · do-calculus · structural causal model · counterfactual · linear SEM · survival and hazard · dynamic regime · E-value · reliability ratio · restricted mean survival time · censoring weights · surrogate endpoint · Z-bias · consistency and coverage

Potential outcomes

Potential outcomes and consistency

Definition. Each unit has one potential outcome for each treatment value: Y(1) if treated and Y(0) if not. Only one of them is observed, the one for the treatment the unit received; the link between the observed outcome and the potential outcomes is called consistency. In this library the observed outcome is usually defined from the potential outcomes and the treatment, so consistency holds by construction; where it is a genuine assumption (as for no anticipation in difference in differences) it is a separate hypothesis.

Example. A unit with Y(1) = 5 and Y(0) = 2 that is treated shows the outcome 5; the value 2 is never seen.

In the library. Randomized.PotentialOutcomes and PotentialOutcomes.observed for a finite population; TwoPeriod.Consistency where consistency is assumed.

Causal effects

Definition. The average treatment effect is E[Y(1) − Y(0)]; the effect on the treated, E[Y(1) − Y(0) | treated]; the complier effect, the same mean among compliers. In a finite population the mean is an average over the units, in a superpopulation an integral against a probability measure.

Example. Four units with Y(1) = (3, 5, 4, 8) and Y(0) = (1, 2, 4, 5) have unit effects (2, 3, 0, 3) and average treatment effect 2.

In the library. PotentialOutcomes.ate, TwoPeriod.att, IVModel.complierEffect; the four units are fourUnitPO.

Identification, estimation and inference

Definition. Identification asks whether a causal quantity is determined by the distribution of the observed data, given the assumptions; an identification theorem writes it as a functional of that distribution. Estimation builds a function of a sample that approximates the functional. Inference quantifies the uncertainty: a test, a p-value, a confidence interval. The library keeps the three apart: identification theorems are statements about distributions, and estimation and inference theorems are about samples or about the randomization of a design.

Example. Under parallel trends the effect on the treated equals the difference in differences of four conditional means (identification). The same difference computed from sample means estimates it, and a confidence interval for it is inference.

In the library. TwoPeriod.att_eq_did (identification); IsConsistent and waldInterval (estimation and inference).

Identified set and point identification

Definition. Given a class of models (those satisfying the assumptions), an observation map and a parameter, the identified set at an observation is the set of parameter values of all models in the class with that observation. The parameter is point identified when every identified set has at most one element, equivalently when it is a function of the observation on the class.

Example. With outcomes in [0, 1], half of the units treated and a treated mean of 0.6, the mean of Y(1) is only known to lie in [0.3, 0.8]: the observed half contributes 0.3, and the unobserved half contributes anything from 0 to 0.5.

In the library. identifiedSet, PointIdentified; the Manski bounds in PartialIdentification.Manski.

Ignorability and positivity

Definition. Ignorability (also called exchangeability or unconfoundedness) says that treatment is independent of the potential outcomes, possibly given covariates. Positivity says that every relevant treatment value has positive probability, so the conditional means used by an identification formula are defined. In the library each is stated with Mathlib's independence and measure notions, as a hypothesis of the theorem that needs it.

Example. The mediation formula needs treatment ignorability for both the outcome and the mediator, and positivity of every cell A = a, M = m that it averages over.

In the library. IVModel.InstrumentIndependence; the hypotheses of integral_nested_eq_mediationFormula.

Designs

Design-based inference

Definition. In the design-based view the potential outcomes of a finite population are fixed numbers, and the only randomness is the assignment of treatment, drawn from a known design. Expectations and variances are taken over the assignments. Neyman's formula gives the variance of the difference in means under complete randomization.

Example. For the four units above with two treated, the six possible assignments are equally likely. The finite population variances are S1² = 14/3, S0² = 10/3 and Sτ² = 2, so Neyman's formula gives (14/3)/2 + (10/3)/2 − 2/4 = 7/2, which is also the variance of the six differences in means around their mean 2.

In the library. Randomized.Design, completeDesign, neyman_variance, and the example fourUnit_variance.

Sharp null and the randomization test

Definition. Fisher's sharp null says that treatment affects no unit: Y(1) = Y(0) for every unit. Under it, the outcomes that would have been observed under any other assignment are known, so the randomization distribution of a test statistic is known exactly, and the p-value is the share of assignments whose statistic is at least the observed one.

Example. With six equally likely assignments, if only the observed assignment reaches the observed statistic, the p-value is 1/6.

In the library. PotentialOutcomes.SharpNull, Design.fisherPValue, fisher_pValue_valid.

Instrument, principal strata and compliers

Definition. A binary instrument Z affects the treatment D, and affects the outcome only through D (the exclusion restriction). Units fall into four principal strata by their potential treatments (D under Z = 0, D under Z = 1): never-takers, always-takers, compliers and defiers. Monotonicity rules out defiers.

Example. In an encouragement trial, compliers take the treatment exactly when encouraged. The Wald ratio estimates their average effect, not the effect in the whole population.

In the library. IVModel, IVModel.Exclusion, IVModel.Monotonicity, IVModel.compliers.

Parallel trends

Definition. In difference in differences, parallel trends says that the mean change of the untreated potential outcome from the pre period to the post period is the same in the treated group as in the control group. No anticipation says that treatment in the post period does not change outcomes in the pre period.

Example. Under staggered adoption the static two-way fixed effects coefficient is a weighted sum of group-time effects that can have negative weights. In the library's example with group-time effects 1, 4 and 1 its closed form gives 1 − 4/2 + 1/2 = −1/2.

In the library. TwoPeriod.ParallelTrends, TwoPeriod.NoAnticipation, EventStudy.twfeCoef.

Regression discontinuity

Definition. Treatment is assigned, or encouraged, when a running variable X crosses a cutoff c. In the sharp design treatment is exactly 1{X ≥ c}; in the fuzzy design the probability of treatment jumps at c. Identification compares the limits of the observed regression of Y on X from the right and from the left of c.

Example. If the observed mean outcome tends to 7 just above the cutoff and to 5 just below, and the untreated and treated mean outcomes are continuous at c, the effect at the cutoff is 2.

In the library. RD.observedRegression, sharp_rd_identification.

Synthetic control

Definition. A single treated unit is compared with a weighted average of untreated donor units, the synthetic control, with nonnegative weights summing to one chosen to fit the treated unit before treatment. The estimated effect is the gap after treatment.

Example. With two donors and weights 0.25 and 0.75, a donor outcome pair (8, 12) gives the synthetic outcome 0.25·8 + 0.75·12 = 11, which always lies between the smallest and largest donor outcome.

In the library. SyntheticControl.synth; the factor model in Designs.SyntheticControl.FactorModel.

Interference

Definition. There is interference when the outcome of a unit depends on the treatment of other units. No interference (part of SUTVA) says that it depends only on the unit's own treatment. Partial interference allows dependence within groups but not across groups. Hudgens and Halloran define direct, indirect, total and overall effects of an allocation strategy.

Example. Two units that respond only when both are treated: the effect of treating one unit is 0 or 1 depending on the other, so a unit level effect is not well defined.

In the library. Interference.NoInterference, Interference.PartialInterference, and the example in Interference.Counterexamples.

Mediation and cross-world quantities

Definition. A mediator M lies on a path from treatment to outcome. The nested potential outcome Y(a, M(a′)) sets treatment to a and the mediator to the value it would take under a′. When a ≠ a′ it combines two worlds that never coexist, so its mean is a cross-world quantity. The natural direct effect compares Y(1, M(0)) with Y(0, M(0)); the natural indirect effect compares Y(a, M(1)) with Y(a, M(0)); the controlled direct effect fixes M = m.

Example. The total effect is the sum of the pure natural direct effect and the total natural indirect effect: TE = NDE(0) + NIE(1).

In the library. MediationModel.nde, MediationModel.nie, MediationModel.cde.

Missing data mechanisms

Definition. With a response indicator R, data are missing completely at random (MCAR) when R is independent of the data, missing at random (MAR) when R is independent of the outcome given observed covariates, and missing not at random (MNAR) otherwise. Under MAR and positivity, inverse probability weighting by the response propensity recovers full-data means.

Example. If half of the outcomes in a stratum of X are observed, each observed outcome in that stratum is weighted by 2.

In the library. MissingData.MCAR, MissingData.MAR, MissingData.MNAR, ipw_identity.

Sensitivity analysis

Definition. A sensitivity analysis asks how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to change a conclusion. In Rosenbaum's model for matched pairs, the odds of treatment of the two units of a pair may differ by at most a factor Γ ≥ 1; Γ = 1 is a randomized paired experiment.

Example. With Γ = 3 the probability that a given unit of a pair is the treated one lies between 1/4 and 3/4.

In the library. Rosenbaum.InSensitivityModel; bias formulas in Sensitivity.BiasFormulas.

Causal graphs

Causal graph, parents and ancestors

Definition. A causal graph is a directed graph whose edge u → v says that u is a direct cause of v. The parents of v are the vertices with an edge into v; u is an ancestor of v when a directed path leads from u to v. The graph is acyclic (a DAG) when no vertex is its own strict ancestor. On a finite vertex set this holds exactly when the vertices can be numbered so that every edge goes up.

Example. In Z → X → Y, X is the only parent of Y and both Z and X are ancestors of Y; numbering Z, X, Y as 0, 1, 2 is a topological order.

In the library. Mathlib's Digraph, with Digraph.parents, Digraph.ancestorsOf, Digraph.IsAcyclic and isAcyclic_iff_exists_adj_lt.

Intervention and graph surgery

Definition. The intervention do(X = x) sets X by external action, which cuts every arrow into X. In the graph this is the surgery that deletes the incoming edges of X; deleting the outgoing edges is the other surgery used by the rules of the do-calculus.

Example. In Z → X → Y with X ← U → Y, the graph of do(X) keeps X → Y and U → Y and deletes Z → X and U → X.

In the library. Digraph.deleteIncoming and its properties in Graphical.Mutilation.

d-separation

Definition. A path between two vertices is blocked by a set S when it has a chain or fork whose middle vertex is in S, or a collider (→ v ←) such that neither v nor any descendant of v is in S. Two sets are d-separated by S when every path between them is blocked. In a distribution that factorizes along the graph, d-separation implies conditional independence.

Example. In X → C ← Y, X and Y are d-separated by the empty set, because the collider C blocks the path; conditioning on C opens it.

In the library. Digraph.DSep, with the decision procedure and examples in the d-separation modules of the Causal graphs area. The link to conditional independence, the global Markov property, is Factorizes.condIndep_of_dSep.

Mixed graphs and districts

Definition. An acyclic directed mixed graph (ADMG) adds bidirected edges u ↔ v to a DAG, standing for a hidden common cause. Its districts (also called c-components) are the classes of vertices connected by bidirected edges.

Example. In the front-door graph X → M → Y with X ↔ Y, the districts are {X, Y} and {M}.

In the library. ADMG, ADMG.district, and checked examples in Graphical.ADMGExamples.

Latent projection

Definition. The latent projection of a graph onto its observed vertices removes the latent vertices and records what they carried: a directed edge u → v for a directed path through latent vertices only, and a bidirected edge u ↔ v for a latent common cause of u and v.

Example. Projecting U out of X ← U → Y, X → Y gives X → Y with X ↔ Y, the bow graph.

In the library. ADMG.project, latentProjection, and the composition law ADMG.project_project.

Moral graph

Definition. The moral graph of a directed graph joins every pair of vertices that share a child ("marrying" the parents) and then forgets the directions of all edges. d-separation can be read off it: restrict the graph to the ancestors of the sets involved, moralize, and check ordinary separation.

Example. In X → C ← Y, given C the ancestral subgraph contains C, its moral graph has an edge between X and Y, and X and Y are not separated. Given the empty set the ancestral subgraph is X and Y alone, with no edge, so they are separated.

In the library. Digraph.dSep_iff_moralSep.

Back-door and front-door criteria

Definition. A set Z satisfies the back-door criterion for the effect of X on Y when no vertex of Z is a descendant of X and Z blocks every path between X and Y that starts with an arrow into X. Then P(y | do(x)) = Σz P(y | x, z) P(z). The front-door criterion covers a mediator set Z that intercepts every directed path from X to Y, when X and Y share an unobserved common cause.

Example. In Z → X → Y with Z → Y, the set {Z} satisfies the back-door criterion; the empty set does not, because of the path X ← Z → Y.

In the library. The modules of the Graphical criteria area, and SCM.effect_eq_sum_of_backDoor and SCM.effect_eq_sum_of_frontDoor.

do-calculus

Definition. Three rules that transform expressions with do(·) into each other when certain d-separations hold in graphs with incoming or outgoing edges deleted: Rule 1 inserts or deletes an observation, Rule 2 exchanges an action and an observation, Rule 3 inserts or deletes an action. An effect is identified when the rules turn it into an expression without do(·).

Example. The back-door adjustment formula is one application of Rule 2.

In the library. SCM.rule1, SCM.rule2, SCM.rule3.

Structural causal model

Definition. A structural causal model assigns to each variable an equation that computes it from its parents in a directed acyclic graph and from its own exogenous noise. With independent noises the model is Markovian, and its distribution factorizes along the graph. The intervention do(X := x) replaces the equations of X by constants; the resulting distribution is the truncated factorization.

Example. In Z → X → Y, the intervention do(X := 1) drops the factor P(X | Z), so P(Z = z, Y = y | do(X := 1)) = P(Z = z) P(Y = y | X = 1).

In the library. The modules of the Structural causal models area, with the truncated factorization SCM.dist_intervene.

Counterfactual

Definition. The potential response Yx(u) is the value of Y in the model modified by do(X := x), at a fixed value u of the noise. The probability of a counterfactual given factual evidence is computed in three steps: abduction (condition the noise on the evidence), action (intervene), and prediction (solve the modified model).

Example. Consistency: if X(u) = x, then intervening to set X to the value it already has changes nothing, so Yx(u) = Y(u).

In the library. SCM.Counterfactuals, with consistency SCM.solve_intervene_of_agree.

Linear structural equation model

Definition. A linear SEM writes each variable as a linear combination of its parents plus an error: X = B X + ε. The entry of B for an edge u → v is the path coefficient, the direct effect of u on v; the total effect is the sum over directed paths of the products of their coefficients. Correlated errors stand for latent common causes.

Example. In Z → X → Y with coefficients a and b, the total effect of Z on Y is a·b, and with uncorrelated errors the regression slope of Y on X is b.

In the library. The modules of the Linear structural equation models area.

Survival, risk and hazard

Definition. For a discrete event time T, the survival at time k is P(T > k), the risk is 1 minus the survival, and the hazard at k is the probability of the event at k among those still at risk. Survival is the product of one minus the hazards. When follow-up is censored, the Kaplan-Meier (product-limit) estimator multiplies the observed proportions surviving each time among those at risk and uncensored.

Example. With hazards 0.1 and 0.2 at the first two times, the survival after two times is 0.9 · 0.8 = 0.72.

In the library. The modules of the Survival analysis area.

Dynamic treatment regime

Definition. A static regime fixes every treatment in advance; a dynamic regime chooses each treatment as a function of the covariates observed up to that time. Its mean outcome is identified by a g-formula in which the treatment at each step is the one the regime assigns to the history.

Example. "Treat at the second visit if and only if the marker is high" is a dynamic regime.

In the library. Longitudinal.expect_eq_gFormulaDyn.

E-value

Definition. For an observed risk ratio RR > 0, let q be the larger of RR and 1/RR. The E-value q + √(q (q − 1)) is the smallest common risk ratio that an unmeasured confounder would need with both the exposure and the outcome to explain away RR completely. The G-value does the same for a confounder of given prevalence among the exposed.

Example. For RR = 4 the E-value is 4 + √12, about 7.46, and a protective RR = 1/4 has the same E-value.

In the library. ExternalAdjustment.boundingFactor_eValue.

Reliability ratio and attenuation

Definition. Under classical measurement error, a surrogate X + e replaces a continuous exposure X, with e uncorrelated with X and with the outcome. The reliability ratio is Var(X) / (Var(X) + Var(e)), between 0 and 1, and the regression slope on the surrogate equals the true slope times the reliability ratio.

Example. If the error variance equals the exposure variance, the reliability ratio is 1/2 and the slope is halved.

In the library. ClassicalError.slope_eq_div_reliability.

Restricted mean survival time

Definition. For an event time T and a horizon τ, the restricted mean survival time is E[min(T, τ)], the area under the survival curve up to τ. In discrete time it is the sum of the survival probabilities at the times before τ.

Example. With survival 1, 0.9 and 0.81 at times 0, 1 and 2, the restricted mean survival time at τ = 3 is 2.71.

In the library. Survival.rmst.

Inverse probability of censoring weights

Definition. When individuals are censored for a reason related to their prognosis, for example at a switch of treatment, each remaining individual is weighted by the inverse of the probability of having remained uncensored given the observed history. The weighted data then stand for the population that would have been observed without censoring.

Example. An individual whose history gave a probability 1/2 of remaining unswitched counts twice.

In the library. Survival.IPCW.ipcWeight.

Surrogate endpoint

Definition. An outcome measured earlier or more easily than the final outcome, used in its place. At the trial level a surrogate is judged by how well the treatment effect on it predicts the effect on the final outcome. An association between surrogate and outcome does not by itself make the surrogate valid: in the surrogate paradox a treatment improves the surrogate, the surrogate improves the outcome, and the treatment still harms the outcome on average.

Example. If the effect on the final outcome is exactly λ₁ times the effect on the surrogate in every trial, a treatment with no effect on the surrogate has no effect on the final outcome.

In the library. Surrogacy.SurrogacyCriteria.

Z-bias

Definition. Adjusting for an instrument, a variable that affects the treatment and affects the outcome only through the treatment, does not remove confounding by an unmeasured variable and can amplify it. In a linear model the bias grows by the factor Var(X) / Var(X | Z).

Example. With every coefficient and error variance equal to 1 the unadjusted bias is 1/3 and the bias after adjusting for the instrument is 1/2.

In the library. ZBias.abs_bias_le.

Estimation

Consistency, asymptotic linearity and coverage

Definition. A sequence of estimators is consistent when it converges in probability to the target. It is asymptotically linear with influence function φ when √n times its error is the scaled sample mean of φ plus a term that vanishes in probability. A confidence interval has asymptotic coverage 1 − α when the probability that it contains the target tends to 1 − α.

Example. With α = 0.05 the Wald interval uses z ≈ 1.96, the point where the standard normal distribution function equals 0.975.

In the library. IsConsistent, IsAsymptoticallyLinear, waldInterval.
